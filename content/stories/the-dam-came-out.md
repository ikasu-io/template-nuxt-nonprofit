By seven in the morning on August 12 there were already lawn chairs on
the bridge. Walt Henning, who has fished above the dam since he was nine,
brought a thermos and a folding table. By eight there were forty people
watching a yellow excavator crawl down the bank.

The first bite took a corner off the spillway. The second opened a notch
two feet wide, and the water behind it, which had sat still since 1923,
started to move.

> "I've fished that pool my whole life. I came to say goodbye to it. I didn't expect to cheer."

## Eleven days

Taking out a dam is slower than people imagine, because you cannot let a
century of silt rush downstream all at once. The crew lowered the water a
foot at a time, scooped out the sediment behind the dam, and spread it on
the banks where it will grow into a floodplain.

On day six the last of the spillway came out. On day nine the crew pulled
out the mill's old intake pipe, which nobody had known was there. On day
eleven they seeded the raw banks and drove away.

## What came back

The creek did not wait. Within a week it had cut its own channel through
the old pool, with riffles, a gravel bar, and a deep run along the
outside bend. In the September fish survey, Marcus found rainbow darters
and a smallmouth bass a mile upstream of the old dam site.

Walt is fishing the new riffle now. He says the bass are smaller and the
fishing is better, which is exactly what a healthy creek looks like.

## Thank you

The dam came out because 1,400 members kept paying their dues through
four years of permits, and because the township, the county and the
Larch Family Fund trusted a small nonprofit with a big job.
